Small Steps Towards a Meaningful Academic Career

You do not build a meaningful career in one day. You build it through small choices, useful habits and the way you treat people. Your academic reputation grows when others can trust your work and your words. This means showing professional integrity, being part of a scholarly community and keeping your intellectual curiosity alive. You do not need to know everything at the beginning. What matters is sustained commitment: keep learning, ask questions and take the next step even when progress feels slow.

It also helps to think about your career prospects without comparing your journey with everyone else’s. Ask for peer support when you need advice, and keep a record of what you are learning. A teaching portfolio, a publication record and a clear research profile can show how you are growing. Set goals that fit your real situation and learn to manage expectations. When you have a busy week, choose one useful task and sustain momentum with steady effort. Every career includes change, so learn to navigate transitions, overcome setbacks and maintain independence while staying open to advice.

You will not always get the result you hoped for. A proposal may be rejected, a paper may need revision, or a new role may feel difficult at first. These moments do not define your future; what you do next matters. When you explain an idea, try to present persuasively by being clear and honest, not by exaggerating. Look for ways to contribute meaningfully to your team, and respond constructively when someone offers feedback. Some academics become internationally recognised, while others make a lasting difference in their classrooms and communities. A highly motivated person is not someone who never feels tired or uncertain. It is someone who remembers what matters, learns from experience and keeps moving forward one step at a time.

1. Matching · 10 points

Choose the noun that completes each adjective–noun or noun–noun collocation. Notice meaning as well as structure.

2. Find the Collocate · 10 points

Complete verb–noun, verb–adverb and adverb–adjective collocations. The correct pattern is not always Verb + Noun.

3. Complete the Sentence · 10 points

Use sentence meaning and grammar to select a collocation that fits the context.

4. Collocation Sorting · 10 points

Identify the structural pattern: Adjective + Noun, Noun + Noun, Verb + Noun, Verb + Adverb or Adverb + Adjective/Participle.

5. Lexical Cloze · 10 points

Choose complete expressions using context, register and meaning rather than word matching alone.

6. SkELL Collocation Investigation · 15 minutes · 10 points (teacher-scored)

Open SkELL. Search portfolio, profile, network and mentoring in Word Sketch. Look for more than Verb + Noun: check adjective + noun, noun + noun, verb + adverb and adverb + adjective patterns where examples support them. Record two collocations, write an original sentence for each, add two related expressions and note how corpus evidence confirmed or changed your first hypothesis.

7. Odd One Out · Based on SkELL · 10 points

Analyse naturalness: identify the expression that does not form a conventional collocation. Some distractors reflect common word-for-word translation errors.

8. Collocation Repair · 10 points

Correct unnatural combinations by considering meaning, grammatical pattern and research context.

9. Progressive Lexical Tests · 24 practice points

These tests become progressively harder: recognise common research collocations, choose expressions in context, distinguish evidence from interpretation, and evaluate realistic research decisions. The practice score is reported separately from the 115-point lesson total.
