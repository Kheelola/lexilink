80-minute lesson route

Teacher-facing timing plan for an 80-minute lesson. The sequence moves from noticing and comprehension to corpus verification, lexical repair and professional writing.

0–3 min · Warm-up Discuss what makes a speaker clear, convincing and respectful.
3–13 min · Reading Read an IELTS-style case study about a local speaking project, a disputed transport proposal and the results of a follow-up survey.
13–17 min · Noticing Study the 20 B2+–C1 collocations, definitions and synonyms.
17–23 min · Recognition Use matching and multiple-choice questions to select collocations that fit the meaning and context.
23–30 min · Context Complete sentences and select expressions that suit speaking and debate scenarios.
30–36 min · Lexical cloze Choose full collocations using context, register and meaning.
36–51 min · DDL with SkELL Test hypotheses about audiences, debates, viewpoints and argument with corpus examples.
51–58 min · Analyse Use Odd One Out to distinguish a synonym group from one collocation with a different meaning.
58–65 min · Repair Correct collocations in communication and professional service scenarios.
65–72 min · Progressive test Move from phrase recognition to meaning in context and more precise collocation choices.
72–80 min · Production Use target collocations to plan a short persuasive talk or respectful debate.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to use B2+–C1 collocations for public speaking and debate, distinguish close synonyms by meaning, test language hypotheses with SkELL, and respond to a different opinion by qualifying a claim or conceding a valid point.

How Civic Voices Changed a Local Debate

The following is a fictional case study created for IELTS-style reading practice.

A | A new public-speaking project

On 14 February 2023, Merebridge Civic Forum announced Civic Voices, a pilot project designed to help residents take part in local decision-making. Thirty-eight people aged between 18 and 70 enrolled. At the opening event on 3 March, communication researcher Dr Amina Shah was invited to deliver a keynote. She explained that a speaker does not need to be dramatic to command attention: a specific example, a clear structure and a well-chosen rhetorical device can make an idea memorable. She added that persuasive techniques should not replace accurate information.

B | Learning to speak in public

Between March and May 2023, the participants attended six workshops led by trainer Lucas Reed. Each person prepared a 90-second talk and practised how to address an audience that might not share their opinions. Reed asked speakers to articulate a position before giving supporting reasons, and to frame an issue fairly rather than hide inconvenient facts. Participants also learnt to hold the floor without interrupting others and to invite questions at the end of a talk. During a public showcase on 20 May, 27 participants gave speeches; organisers reported that most stayed within the time limit.

C | A proposal divides the town

The skills were soon tested. On 12 October 2023, the council proposed turning 46 parking spaces at Old Mill Square into a bus interchange. Supporters said the new service could reduce journey times, while opponents worried about access to nearby shops and the health centre. The proposal began to spark controversy, producing a polarised debate at a public meeting attended by 126 residents. When shop owner Peter Lang and councillor Beth Morgan disagreed about the expected loss of customers, their disagreement turned into a heated exchange. Morgan then raised an objection, arguing that the revised bus stops could leave some patients with a 400-metre walk to the health centre.

D | From confrontation to discussion

Forum chair Daniel Okafor intervened to steer the discussion back to evidence. Transport officer Leah Chen used a map and journey-time figures to counter an objection about access. Morgan conceded a point: she accepted that the planned crossing would improve safety, although she still opposed the loss of parking spaces. Deputy mayor Colin Harris was then accused of withholding a traffic report. He attempted to rebut a charge by referring to the council’s published minutes, which recorded when the report had been received. Harris also had to qualify a statement about a similar scheme in Fairhaven: he explained that local conditions were different, even though the council there had consulted residents before making a decision. He went on to cite a precedent from Fairhaven’s 2021 consultation process, not as proof that the same plan would work in Merebridge, but as an example of a process the town could adapt.

E | What the project achieved

On 19 February 2024, the forum introduced new meeting rules: speakers would receive equal time, each claim about public services should be supported by a source where possible, and the chair would summarise unresolved questions before a vote. The rules encouraged a more measured response to disagreement. At the closing event on 28 May 2024, participant Mina Yusuf described how her family’s daily journey to the health centre had influenced her view. Her account helped trigger applause, even among people who still opposed the interchange. A report published on 30 May recorded 92 completed questionnaires from people who had attended the project’s final three events. Of the respondents, 68% said they felt more confident asking questions in public, while 61% said they were more willing to listen to an unfamiliar argument. The authors warned that the questionnaire was voluntary and could not show that the project alone had caused these changes. They concluded only that the results were encouraging and that further evaluation was needed.

IELTS-style Reading Task 3 — Multiple Choice

Choose the best answer according to the passage.

1. Matching · 10 points

Complete each expression using its meaning and the context; choose the word that makes a natural collocation.

2. Find the Collocate · 10 points

Choose the missing word in each collocation. Read the whole expression, not just the blank.

3. Complete the Sentence · 10 points

Use sentence meaning and grammar to select a collocation that fits the context.

4. Multiple Choice · 10 points

Choose the collocation that best fits the speaker’s purpose and the situation. Focus on meaning and context, not parts of speech.

5. Lexical Cloze · 10 points

Choose the expression that fits the situation and the meaning of the sentence.

6. SkELL Collocation Investigation · 15 minutes · 10 points (teacher-scored)

Open SkELL. Search keynote, objection, discussion and response in Word Sketch. Compare examples and meanings; do not classify expressions by parts of speech. Record two collocations, write an original sentence for each, add two related expressions and note how corpus evidence confirmed or changed your first hypothesis.

7. Odd One Out · Based on SkELL · 10 points

Three options in each question express similar meanings. Choose the one collocation that does NOT belong to the synonym group. All options are natural expressions; the odd one out is different in meaning.

8. Collocation Meaning Check · 10 points

Choose the collocation that matches the meaning described. All options are natural English expressions; use meaning rather than parts of speech.

9. Progressive Lexical Tests · 24 practice points

These tests become progressively harder: recognise collocations, choose expressions in context, compare close meanings, and evaluate realistic speaking and debate situations. The practice score is reported separately from the 115-point lesson total.
